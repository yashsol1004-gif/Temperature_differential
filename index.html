Stress in the slab

Each hour’s dT is turned into a bending stress. The wheel load stress is added where it pulls the same face of the slab, and the total is divided by the flexural strength to give the stress ratio SR. Everything else comes from IRC:58 and is listed at the bottom of this page.

How it works

1

Heat moves down through the slab

Conduction carries heat from the hot surface into the cooler concrete below.

∂T/∂t = α · ∂²T/∂z² α = k / (ρ · cp)  —  the thermal diffusivity
2

The sun and the wind act on the top surface

Sunlight heats the surface. The wind and the night sky carry heat away again.

hc    = 5.6 + 4.0 · wind q_net = 0.6 · solar − hc · (T_top − T_air) − 66
3

Every point steps forward one minute at a time

The slab is cut into points about 10 mm apart, from the top face down to the bottom one. A 320 mm slab gets 33 of them.

T(i, t+Δt) = T(i, t) + r · [ T(i+1, t) − 2T(i, t) + T(i−1, t) ]

Values used

k 1.8 W/m·K how well concrete carries heat ρ2400 kg/m³ density of concrete cp 920 J/kg·K specific heat of concrete α8.15 × 10−7 m²/sworked out from k / (ρ · cp) Δzabout 10 mm gap between the depth points, whatever the thickness Δt60 s one time step r α · Δt / Δz² must be 0.5 or less, which is why thin slabs are refused

Two assumptions I made

  • The same day is repeated 8 times, so the slab forgets the temperature it was started at.
  • No heat crosses the bottom face of the slab.

How the stress is worked out

4

How far the slab reaches out to the ground below

A stiff slab on a soft subgrade spreads its load over a longer length. That length is the radius of relative stiffness, l.

l = [ E h³ / (12 (1 − μ²) k) ]1/4
5

How much of the curling the slab's own weight holds down

Bradbury's coefficient. A short slab lifts freely and bends little, so C is small. A long slab is held down by its own weight and bends fully, so C nears 1.

C = 1 − 2 (sinλ coshλ + cosλ sinhλ) / (sin2λ + sinh2λ) λ = side / (l √8)  —  the slab length L for Cx, the lane width W for Cy
6

The two stresses

Westergaard for the wheel, Bradbury for the temperature. The wheel load stress is the same all day; the thermal stress changes every hour with dT.

edge   = 0.572 P / h² × [ 4 log₁₀(l/b) + 0.359 ] corner = 3 P / h² × [ 1 − (a√2 / l)0.6 ] thermal = Cx · E · α · dT / 2 b = √(1.6a² + h²) − 0.675h, and a is the tyre contact radius
7

Adding them up, and the stress ratio

They are added only where they pull the same face. At the edge the wheel load and a positive dT both pull the bottom, so they add by day. At the corner the wheel load and a negative dT both pull the top, so they add at night.

edge, bottom  = edge   + thermal corner, top   = corner − thermal SR = total / fcr a negative total means compression, so it is set to zero

What this part assumes

  • dT is the plain top minus bottom temperature, not the equivalent linear gradient. The real profile is curved, so this slightly overstates dT.
  • Bradbury gives no corner coefficient, so Cx is used at the corner as well. This is the usual approximation, but it is still an approximation.
  • No cumulative fatigue damage is worked out, because that needs a design traffic figure. Only SR and the allowed repetitions N are given.
  • The subgrade modulus k is not yet fixed. It needs a CBR and a subbase read off IRC:58 Tables 2 to 4, so it is left as a box to type into.
